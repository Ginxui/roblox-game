Topic 1

Order of Operations

The short version

Math has rules for what to do first. If everyone follows the same rules, everyone gets the same answer.

Remember: PEMDAS

“Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally.” Do them in this order:

PParenthesesbrackets ( )
EExponentslittle numbers up top, like 5²
M DMultiply & Divide× ÷
A SAdd & Subtract+ −

Big trick: × and ÷ are a tie. Do the one on the left first. Same thing for + and −.

Example A
3 + 4 × 2
  1. 3 + 4 × 2Multiply before you add
  2. 3 + 8Now add
Answer: 11
Example B
20 ÷ 4 × 5
  1. 20 ÷ 4 × 5÷ and × tie, so start on the left
  2. 5 × 5Now multiply
Answer: 25
Example C
(2 + 3)² − 4 × 3
  1. (2 + 3)² − 4 × 3P: do the brackets first
  2. 5² − 4 × 3E: 5² means 5 × 5 = 25
  3. 25 − 4 × 3M: multiply
  4. 25 − 12S: subtract
Answer: 13
Example D
6 + 2 × (5 − 1)² ÷ 4
  1. 6 + 2 × 4² ÷ 4P: 5 − 1 = 4
  2. 6 + 2 × 16 ÷ 4E: 4² = 16
  3. 6 + 32 ÷ 4M: 2 × 16 = 32
  4. 6 + 8D: 32 ÷ 4 = 8
Answer: 14
Common mistake

In 3 + 4 × 2, don’t add first. 7 × 2 = 14 is wrong! Multiply first: 3 + 8 = 11.

Try it yourself

10 − 2 × 3
Multiply first: 2 × 3 = 6. Then 10 − 6 = 4.
18 ÷ 3 × 2
Left to right: 18 ÷ 3 = 6, then 6 × 2 = 12.
(8 − 3)² + 1
Brackets: 8 − 3 = 5. Then 5² = 25. Then 25 + 1 = 26.
2 + 3 × (4 + 6) ÷ 5
Brackets: 4 + 6 = 10. Then 3 × 10 = 30. Then 30 ÷ 5 = 6. Last, 2 + 6 = 8.
Topic 2

Evaluating Expressions

The short version

A letter like x is a stand-in for a number. “Evaluate” just means put the number where the letter is, then do the math.

How to do it

  1. Write the problem down.
  2. Swap each letter for its number. Put the number in brackets, like (4).
  3. Solve it using PEMDAS from Topic 1.

Good to know: 3x means 3 × x. A number next to a letter means multiply.

Example A · x = 4
3x + 5
  1. 3(4) + 5Swap x for 4
  2. 12 + 5Multiply, then add
Answer: 17
Example B · x = 7
5(x − 2)
  1. 5(7 − 2)Swap x for 7
  2. 5(5)Brackets first: 7 − 2 = 5
Answer: 25
Example C · a = 3, b = 5
2a² − b
  1. 2(3)² − (5)Swap a for 3 and b for 5
  2. 2(9) − 53² = 3 × 3 = 9
  3. 18 − 5Multiply, then subtract
Answer: 13
Example D · x = −3
x² + 2x
  1. (−3)² + 2(−3)Swap x for −3. Keep it in brackets!
  2. 9 + (−6)−3 × −3 = 9, and 2 × −3 = −6
Answer: 3

Real-life example

A pizza costs $10, plus $2 for each topping. So the cost is 10 + 2t. The t stands for how many toppings. What if you get 3 toppings?

  1. 10 + 2(3) = 10 + 6Swap t for 3, then multiply
Cost: $16
Common mistake

If x = −3, then x² is (−3) × (−3) = 9. The answer is not −9. Two negatives multiplied make a positive.

Try it yourself

4n − 1, when n = 6
4(6) − 1 = 24 − 1 = 23.
x² + 3, when x = 5
(5)² + 3 = 25 + 3 = 28.
2(a + b), when a = 4, b = 1
2(4 + 1) = 2(5) = 10.
10 − 3y, when y = −2
10 − 3(−2) = 10 − (−6) = 10 + 6 = 16.
Topic 3

Comparing Fractions

The short version

Think of a fraction as a pizza. The bottom number is how many slices the pizza is cut into. The top number is how many slices you get.

Three easy cases

  1. Same bottom? The bigger top wins. 5/8 > 3/8, because 5 slices is more than 3 slices.
  2. Same top? The smaller bottom wins. 2/3 > 2/5, because a pizza cut into 3 has bigger slices than one cut into 5.
  3. Both numbers different? Use the butterfly trick below.

See it: 2/3 vs 2/5

2/3
2/5

You get 2 slices each time, but the slices on top are bigger. So 2/3 is bigger.

The butterfly trick

Multiply in an X shape: each bottom number times the top number on the other side. Write each answer on the side of the top number. The side with the bigger answer wins.

3 × 7 = 21 34 vs 57 5 × 4 = 20

21 is bigger than 20, so 3/4 > 5/7.

Example A
3/8 ? 5/8
  1. Same bottom (8)So look at the tops: 3 is less than 5
Answer: 3/8 < 5/8
Example B
1/4 ? 1/6
  1. Same top (1)Smaller bottom wins: 4 is less than 6
Answer: 1/4 > 1/6
Example C · make the bottoms match
5/12 ? 1/2
  1. 1/2 = 6/12Times the top and bottom by 6
  2. 5/12 vs 6/12Now the bottoms match: 5 is less than 6
Answer: 5/12 < 1/2
Example D · butterfly
4/9 ? 3/5
  1. 4 × 5 = 20Left top × right bottom
  2. 3 × 9 = 27Right top × left bottom. 27 is bigger!
Answer: 4/9 < 3/5

Calculator trick

Type the top number ÷ the bottom number. 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75 and 5 ÷ 7 = 0.71… Since 0.75 is bigger, 3/4 wins.

Try it yourself

7/10 ? 3/10
Same bottom, and 7 is more than 3. So 7/10 is bigger.
3/7 ? 3/4
Same top, so the smaller bottom wins. 3/4 is bigger.
2/3 ? 5/8
Butterfly: 2 × 8 = 16 and 5 × 3 = 15. 16 is bigger, so 2/3 is bigger.
Order from least to greatest: 1/2, 2/5, 3/4
On a calculator: 1/2 = 0.5, 2/5 = 0.4, 3/4 = 0.75. So the order is 2/5, 1/2, 3/4.
Topic 4

Rounding

The short version

Rounding makes a number easier to use. Look at the number next door (on the right). 5 or more: go up. 4 or less: stay the same.

How to do it

  1. Find the spot you’re rounding to (like the tens spot). 347
  2. Look next door, one spot to the right. 347
  3. 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 → add 1 to your spot. 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 → don’t change it.
  4. Clean up: numbers to the right turn into 0. After a decimal point, just drop them.

A rhyme to help: “Five or more, raise the score. Four or less, let it rest.”

Picture it on a number line

347 is closer to 350 than to 340. So it rounds to 350.

Example A · nearest ten
347
  1. 347Next door is 7, so the 4 goes up to 5
Answer: 350
Example B · nearest hundred
3,462
  1. 3,462Next door is 6, so the 4 goes up to 5
Answer: 3,500
Example C · nearest tenth
2.748
  1. 2.748Next door is 4, so the 7 stays
Answer: 2.7
Example D · tricky one
6.95 → nearest tenth
  1. 6.95Next door is 5, so the 9 goes up
  2. 9 + 1 = 109 goes up to 10, so the 6 turns into a 7
Answer: 7.0

Topic 5

Number Systems

The short version

Numbers belong to groups. The groups fit inside each other, like boxes inside boxes. A number can be in more than one group.

Natural numbers

The numbers you count with.

1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …

Integers

Whole numbers. Zero and negatives count too. No fractions or decimals.

…, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …

Rational numbers

Any number you can write as a fraction. Its decimal either stops or repeats.

1/2, −4, 0.75, 0.333…, 5

Irrational numbers

Decimals that go on forever and never repeat. You can’t write them as a fraction.

π = 3.14159…, √2 = 1.41421…

Watch out: √9 looks hard, but √9 = 3 (because 3 × 3 = 9). So it’s a natural number!

Quick check for any number

  1. Is it a counting number (1, 2, 3…)? → Natural. It’s also an integer and rational.
  2. Is it 0 or a negative whole number? → Integer. It’s also rational.
  3. Is it a fraction, or a decimal that stops or repeats? → Rational.
  4. Does the decimal go on forever with no pattern? → Irrational.

Try it yourself

−12
Integer and rational. It’s not natural, because you can’t count with negatives.
2.5
Rational. The decimal stops. It’s the same as 5/2.
√16
√16 = 4 (because 4 × 4 = 16). So it’s natural, integer, and rational.
√5
Irrational. No whole number times itself makes 5. Its decimal (2.2360…) never stops or repeats.
0.121212…
Rational. The “12” repeats over and over.
